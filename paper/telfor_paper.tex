\title{Null Models and Sampling-Based Evaluation Expose the Real Value of
Reinforcement Learning for LLVM Pass Ordering}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Dimitrije Pe\v{s}i\'c}
\IEEEauthorblockA{\textit{[Afilijacija]}\\
Belgrade, Serbia\\
dimitrije.pesicc@gmail.com}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Reinforcement learning (RL) for compiler pass ordering is commonly
evaluated by comparing a deterministic policy rollout against a fixed
optimization level such as \texttt{-O3}. We show that both choices can
reverse conclusions. We introduce two null models defined in the same
curated 36-pass action space that the learned policies act in---a single
random episode and best-of-$N$ random search---and evaluate policies by
sampling rather than by their argmax mode. Across 380~programs from seven
independent sources, random search in the curated space alone beats
\texttt{-Oz} on every real-code suite (1.6--24.5\%), while a graph-neural
policy trained on only four small programs beats the budget-matched
random null on 24 of 24 suite--seed combinations --- significant within
every suite (one-sided Wilcoxon, largest $p{=}0.025$) --- reaches
exhaustive greedy-search quality at 73\% of its compilation budget, and produces \texttt{.text} sections 10.6\%
smaller than \texttt{-Oz} with pass sequences that apply faster than the
\texttt{-Oz} pipeline itself. An identically trained flat-feature policy
achieves neither. Finally, RL gradients alone fail to train the graph
encoder; distilling hand-crafted features into it before RL removes this
failure. Measurement choices, not representation choices, dominate the
reported conclusions in this domain.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
compiler optimization, LLVM, phase ordering, reinforcement learning,
graph neural networks, evaluation methodology
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
The order in which a compiler applies its optimization passes changes
the quality of the generated code, and choosing a good order per program
--- the \emph{phase-ordering problem} --- is NP-hard. Fixed pipelines such
as LLVM's \texttt{-O3} and \texttt{-Oz} apply one hand-tuned sequence to
every program, which makes program-specific orderings an attractive
target for machine learning, and a line of work has applied deep
reinforcement learning to the problem~\cite{autophase,compilergym,posetrl,coreset}.

Reported results in this area, however, rest on two evaluation
conventions that are rarely questioned. First, learned policies are
compared against fixed optimization levels --- often \texttt{-O3}, a
\emph{speed}-oriented pipeline, even when the objective is code
\emph{size} --- rather than against a null model that measures how much of
the gain is available without any learning. Second, policies are
evaluated by a single deterministic (argmax) rollout, which measures the
mode of the policy distribution rather than the distribution itself.

This paper asks what remains of an RL pass-ordering system once both
conventions are replaced. We train proximal policy optimization
(PPO)~\cite{ppo} agents with two state representations --- the flat
56-dimensional Autophase feature vector~\cite{autophase} and a custom
GraphSAGE~\cite{graphsage} encoder over control- and data-flow graphs
parsed from LLVM IR --- under a controlled protocol in which the
representation is the only variable, and we evaluate them on
380~programs drawn from seven independent sources against null models
defined in the same action space and with the same step budget.

Our contributions are threefold. (1)~We show that a curated 36-pass
action space is itself the dominant effect: best-of-50 random search
inside it matches greedy search and beats \texttt{-Oz} on every
real-code suite, a fact that any evaluation in this domain should
control for. (2)~We show that the evaluation mode reverses the ranking
of representations: under argmax rollouts the two agents are
indistinguishable, while under best-of-$k$ sampling the graph-based
policy beats the paired random null on every one of 24 suite--seed
combinations and reaches exhaustive greedy-search quality at 73\% of its
compilation budget --- a property the flat-feature policy does not have.
(3)~We show that RL gradients alone fail to train the graph encoder,
and that distilling Autophase features into the encoder before RL
removes the failure, improving deterministic-rollout generalization by
9--10\%. All results are reproducible from a public repository with
pinned dependencies.\footnote{\texttt{https://github.com/dimitrijepesic/compiler-opt}}

\section{Related Work}
AutoPhase~\cite{autophase} introduced deep RL over a flat feature vector
for phase ordering; CompilerGym~\cite{compilergym} standardized the
environment we build on. ProGraML~\cite{programl} showed that graph
representations of programs outperform flat features on supervised
compiler tasks, and MLGO~\cite{mlgo} deployed learned policies inside
production LLVM for inlining and register allocation. POSET-RL~\cite{posetrl}
and the coreset approach of Liang et al.~\cite{coreset} reduce the
action space before learning; the latter is the closest work to ours,
selecting from a coreset of 50 pass sequences with a GNN and reporting
an average 4.7\% improvement over \texttt{-Oz}. Our study is
complementary: rather than proposing a new agent, it measures
\emph{where such gains come from}, using null models defined inside the
same reduced space --- a control absent from prior evaluations --- and
shows that the decoding mode (argmax vs.\ sampling) changes conclusions.
Mammadli et al.~\cite{mammadli} observed that random search is a strong
competitor to RL in this domain; we formalize that observation into an
evaluation protocol and replicate it across seven suites. Large language
models have also been applied to this problem~\cite{llmcompiler}, at a
very different computational budget.

\section{Experimental Protocol}
\textbf{Environment and metric.} We use CompilerGym~0.2.5
(\texttt{llvm-ic-v0}, LLVM~10)~\cite{compilergym}. The optimization
objective is LLVM IR instruction count (IC); Section~\ref{sec:binary}
validates IC against actual binary section sizes.
\texttt{-O3}/\texttt{-Oz} baselines are the environment's real
optimization-level observations, not approximated pass lists.

\textbf{Action space.} Profiling all 124 available passes on the
training programs yields 36 passes that each improve at least one
program; these cover 100\% of the observed single-pass improvement.
All agents, null models, and searches operate in this space with
45-step episodes.

\textbf{Agents and training.} Both agents use PPO (clip 0.2,
$\gamma{=}0.99$, GAE $\lambda{=}0.95$) with truncation-aware advantage
bootstrapping, KL-based early stopping of update epochs (target 0.02),
and a linearly decaying entropy coefficient. The Autophase agent
observes a $\log(1{+}x)$-transformed 56-dimensional feature vector; the
GNN agent parses the IR into a graph (one-hot opcode plus four scalar
features per instruction; separate control-flow and data-flow edge
types) and encodes it with a three-layer edge-type-aware GraphSAGE
network. Both train on the \emph{same} four small cBench programs
($<$3000 instructions), for the same 100\,000 environment steps, with
three seeds each; checkpoints are selected on a three-program validation
subset. Apart from the joint optimizer that the shared trainable encoder
requires (encoder learning rate $10^{-4}$), the representation is the
only variable.

\textbf{Null models.} Two null models are measured in the same 36-pass
space: (a)~a single random 45-step episode (the null for one policy
rollout) and (b)~the best of $N$ random episodes (the null for search).
When a policy is evaluated by best-of-$k$ sampling, its null is the best
of the \emph{same number} $k$ of stored random episodes on the same
program --- paired by construction.

\textbf{Evaluation.} Policies are evaluated in two modes: a single
deterministic argmax rollout, and best-of-$k$ stochastic rollouts
sampled from the policy distribution (GNN rollouts sample with the
encoder's dropout active, which adds exploration noise). The evaluation battery spans
seven sources: cBench (val{+}test, 9), MiBench~\cite{mibench} (40),
CHStone~\cite{chstone} (12), NPB (122), BLAS (50), a 97-program POJ-104
sample, and 50 csmith-generated~\cite{csmith} synthetic programs ---
371~battery programs plus cBench, none seen in training. Battery
policy evaluations use programs up to 6000 instructions (347 of 371)
to bound graph-extraction cost.

\textbf{Encoder pretraining.} In one arm, the GNN encoder is first
trained to regress the Autophase vector from the program graph (2430
states collected by random episodes on the training programs) and then
fine-tuned with RL.

\section{Results}

\subsection{The curated action space, not learning, is the main effect}
Table~\ref{tab:nulls} compares real optimization levels against
best-of-50 random episodes in the 36-pass space. Random search beats
\texttt{-Oz} on all six real-code suites, by 1.6\% (MiBench) up to
24.5\% (NPB); on NPB even a \emph{single} random episode beats
\texttt{-Oz} by 11\% on average. Two boundaries are equally
consistent: \texttt{-O3} is the wrong yardstick for size (on small
programs it \emph{increases} IC above \texttt{-O0}), and on synthetic
csmith programs the full \texttt{-Oz} pipeline retains a 5.2\%
advantage --- the curated space was distilled from real code and
transfers across real code only. Any learned-policy result in this
domain should therefore be read relative to these nulls, not to
optimization levels alone.

\subsection{Sampling reverses the ranking of representations}
Under argmax rollouts, both trained policies transfer poorly to programs
one to two orders of magnitude larger than their training set --- the GNN
policy's deterministic mode degenerates (e.g., 79\,686 total IC on NPB
against an \texttt{-Oz} of 53\,085) --- and the two representations are
statistically indistinguishable. Sampling the same checkpoints changes
the picture completely. Table~\ref{tab:sampling} reports best-of-8
sampled rollouts against the paired best-of-8 random null: the GNN
policy beats its null on \emph{every} suite--seed combination --- 24 of
24 across the seven sources. Because the three seeds within a suite are
compared against the same stored null episodes, we test at the level of
the eight independent suite/split units: the GNN policy wins all eight
under every seed (exact sign test $p{=}3.9{\times}10^{-3}$); one-sided
Wilcoxon tests against the per-program nulls are significant within
every suite (largest $p{=}0.025$ on MiBench, down to $p{<}10^{-5}$ on
NPB and POJ-104); and re-pairing each seed with a disjoint slice of the
stored episodes, which restores independence across all 24 pairs,
leaves the count at 24 of 24. The identically trained Autophase policy
wins 15 of 24 pairs, two of the eight units, and is significant on four
of the seven suites. The margin over the null is
modest (1--2\%) but perfectly systematic, and it holds on the synthetic
csmith programs where pure random search \emph{loses} to \texttt{-Oz}:
there the sampled GNN policy is 16\% below \texttt{-Oz}. A policy
trained on four toy programs thus carries transferable ordering
knowledge to seven foreign program sources --- but only sampling can see
it.

front on cBench. At $k{=}8$ (360 compilations per program) the sampled

seeds beat the paired null. Within the measured budgets random search
never catches the sampler on cBench; on the battery it does so only at
$k{=}32$, and first on NPB --- precisely the suite where random search
is strongest. The learned sampler, in other words, buys down the cost
of search: it dominates the front at the budgets where search is
expensive.

\subsection{Pretraining unlocks the encoder}
All three from-scratch GNN seeds converge to the same validation
plateau (689), the value a nearly untrained policy already reaches: RL
gradients alone do not teach the encoder useful features. Distilling
Autophase into the encoder before RL (a one-hour CPU job) removes the
failure in two of two seeds (Table~\ref{tab:pretrain}), and improves
full-split deterministic-rollout totals by 9--10\% on average, with the better
seed within 0.5\% of \texttt{-Oz} --- the first deterministic policy in
the study to beat the single-episode random null on both held-out
splits. Pretraining repairs the policy's \emph{mode}; the from-scratch
policy remains the better \emph{sampler}. The two fixes are
complementary: pretrain when only one rollout is affordable, sample
when eight are.

\subsection{Binary-level metrics}
\label{sec:binary}
IC is a proxy, so we compiled the optimized modules to objects with the
environment's own LLVM~10 code generator and measured section sizes.
On programs with at least 1000 instructions, relative IC reduction
strongly predicts relative \texttt{.text} reduction (Pearson
$r{=}0.78$, $p{=}2.2{\times}10^{-6}$); below that size, fixed
code-generation overheads dominate and the proxy weakens --- a boundary
we report rather than hide. The IC gains translate into bytes: across
the 347 paired battery programs, best-of-8 GNN sampling produces
\texttt{.text} sections 10.6\% smaller than \texttt{-Oz} (864\,887
vs.\ 967\,878 bytes; the random null achieves 10.4\%), and across all
371 programs random search alone is 4.0\% below \texttt{-Oz}. The
discovered 45-pass sequences also apply \emph{faster} than the
\texttt{-Oz} pipeline (median 15.0--15.8\,ms vs.\ 19.5\,ms per module
through the plain \texttt{opt} CLI, which also demonstrates the
sequences work outside the RL environment). Total object footprint is
dominated ($>$99.9\%) by data and BSS sections, which are essentially
invariant under pass ordering ($<$0.03\% change), so \texttt{.text} is
the informative size metric.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
Three practices follow from these results. Evaluations of learned pass
ordering should (1)~report a random null inside the same action space
and budget, since a curated space plus trivial search already beats
\texttt{-Oz}; (2)~report sampling-based in addition to deterministic
evaluation, since the two can reverse rankings; and (3)~never use
\texttt{-O3} as a size baseline. Our own limitations are measured
rather than hypothetical: the study optimizes IR instruction count with
a validated but size-bounded correlation to binary sections; training
used four small programs by design (as the experimental control), and
a mixed-size training arm did not improve generalization; runtime
performance is not measured; and the stack is CompilerGym~0.2.5
(LLVM~10). The per-step cost of the GNN representation
($\sim$17$\times$ the flat features during training) is real, though at
deployment the cost of a sampled rollout is dominated by compilation
itself.

\section{Conclusion}
Under controlled measurement, the value of RL for LLVM pass ordering
redistributes: most of the headline gain over \texttt{-Oz} belongs to
the curated action space; the genuine, statistically unambiguous
contribution of the learned graph policy is that of an \emph{amortized
searcher} --- greedy-search quality at a fraction of its compilation
budget, transferable from four toy programs to seven foreign suites ---
and the encoder that carries it can only be trained with a pretraining
step that costs an hour of CPU. Evaluation choices dominated
representation choices at every step of this study; we suggest treating
that as the default hypothesis in this domain.
